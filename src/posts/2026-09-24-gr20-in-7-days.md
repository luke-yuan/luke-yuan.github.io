## Intro

I went into this trip with very minimal knowledge of the GR20 and Corsica. I read some blog posts and watched some videos about speedrunning the GR20, and brushed away most warnings that it was exceptionally difficult—I run a lot, love the outdoors, and consider myself athletic. I loaded the GPX maps onto my Garmin and had vague notions the terrain would be challenging, but it’s just walking, how hard could it be?

## Preparation

I decided to do this trip in late August (**August 23–29, 2026**) in the **south → north** direction. I chose the reverse of the popular north → south direction, with the simple reason of not having to face the sun during most of the hike. I had all hotels and tents booked for my stay in Corsica, and other than that and my flights, I did not do much in terms of preparation.

I did not do any special training for this trip—I was pretty confident as I already run a good distance every week. Looking back, I don’t know how I would’ve prepared differently—I don’t think doing a long day hike would’ve really helped much. A year prior to this I did the Alta Via 1 with a friend, but did not double any stages, which turned out to be easy and made me irrationally confident about the GR20.

## Arrival

The starting point of the GR20 if you do it south → north is in the small and remote town of Conca. The nearest airport reachable by commercial airlines is Figari (FSC), where I arrived on an Air Corsica plane via Paris Orly. Getting around Corsica without a car is generally difficult, and that starts even at the airport. I had booked a taxi in advance since my flight had a late arrival time, but you can also try calling local taxi operators after you land, although availability does not seem to be guaranteed. I used the local operator [Taxi Flo](https://taxi-figari.fr/) (because they had a website form you could reserve taxis with), but after landing I found the fixed rates of [Taxi Ciabrini](https://taxi-portovecchio.com/) much cheaper.

I spent the night in Porto-Vecchio, the nearest city on the way to Conca. Hotels and hostels are generally pretty expensive during the peak summer season.

After getting up, I spent a day exploring Porto-Vecchio. This was probably the most uneventful day of the trip—I had thought I would be able to visit one of the beaches, but getting around turned out to be quite annoying without a car, so I gathered some last-minute supplies: **cash**, hiking poles from Decathlon and sunscreen from the pharmacy nearby and chilled around until my scheduled taxi to Conca. It would’ve been much more convenient if Conca had a car rental drop-off location so I could’ve rented a car instead, but alas. Taxi costs alone from Figari to Porto-Vecchio and Porto-Vecchio to Conca ran to €230. Uber can’t come sooner to the Mediterranean island.

I decided to stay in the [Hôtel San Pasquale](https://maps.app.goo.gl/t3VECmZP4dLxfyun7), one of the two options you have in Conca, the other being [Gîte de la Tonnelle](https://maps.app.goo.gl/FzUXNhgf1KUuSERm8). My hotel was very bare bones (early taste of mountain lodging) but it was still a comfortable experience: the owner was very kind and there was a restaurant on site that served pizzas.

Coming from the US on an extended France trip that would continue after the GR20, I had extra non-hiking-related luggage with me, and I was happy to find out luggage transport services like Cinto Transport existed. I dropped off a suitcase at their drop-off location in Conca, in Gîte de la Tonnelle, and the process was easy and smooth.

## Day 1: Conca → Asinau

The first morning I woke up early due to jet lag and was pretty hyped up to start. I had forgotten the hotel served breakfast, and thinking that I already had some snacks with me, I decided to skip and hit the trail directly. The first half of the day was very easy: I reached the Refuge d’I Paliri at 10 AM and decided to have an early lunch there. I thought the rest of the hike would be easy and almost started having thoughts about doing the GR20 in less than 7 days.

### Refuge Asinau Review

When I reached Asinau, I was pretty shot and asked for unhealthy snacks and an Orangina; classic American behavior. I also hadn’t realized how simple the mountain refuges were, my only hut-to-hut hiking experience being the Alta Via 1. I was pretty shocked to realize there was only one hot shower in the entire refuge. I arrived at around 16h30 and thought I had lots of time to take a shower and wash my clothes, but factoring in the line and everything else, I did not get much rest until the dinner service at 18h30. Dinners at refuges on the GR20 are pretty communal and so you will be expected to share tables with strangers, especially if you are a solo traveller like me. It’s one of the best parts of the trip for me as I get to practice my French and learn what the next days will be like as most people do this trip north → south.

The tent setup here was okay but it being my first time sleeping in a tent in a long while, I did not find it to be very comfortable (shocker) and ended up waking up early. By the way, all tents here come with a mattress, so there is no need to pack one yourself.

This refuge offers breakfast which I ordered, but it was very simple “biscottes” with jam and was definitely not worth the euros I spent. However, I still ordered breakfast for the rest of the trip, because laziness.

## Day 2: Asinau → Prati

Looking back, this day was the hardest mentally for me. Most people that have done the GR20 might find this hard to believe since the southern section (south of Vizzavona) is known to be “easy”; however, the first day had given me a false sense of confidence.

As recommended by my dinner mates, I got up super early this day and managed to start at 6 AM. I realized an issue once I got up: I hadn’t got a headlamp! I was a bit concerned about this but realized effectively my phone flashlight would ultimately work. It sure did—and fortunately if you do this trip in summer you won’t have to be using it for long, as the sun begins to rise. The day started with a long climb and the first half to Usciolu was actually in line with my first day, and I reached the refuge at around 12:30 and had a quick lunch.

It was the afternoon that I started to struggle a bit. I had only been focusing on the distance metric of my watch and assumed walking conditions would be easy, but on this part of the hike the terrain started getting technical and my pace was forced to slow down by a lot, as technical terrain was unfamiliar to me. What I thought would have been an 8-hour day eventually grew to almost 10, and I was certain of quitting after day 3.

### Refuge Prati Review

Despite its negative Google reviews, Prati was actually my favorite refuge of the trip. It offered a fully indoor dinner/rest area which I did not often encounter in refuges during the rest of the trip. Its tents were clean, workers were friendly, and dinner had good portions of meat. The night we stayed we encountered some great winds and rain, but my tent held up well.

I met some seasoned GR20 walkers here, and they all rallied me to not give up. They showed me the official guide times of the hike I had done that day and showed me that I was still really quick—it was just that I was basing my expectations of walking times on easy trails rather than scrambles. I lightened up a lot that night and knew I’d finish the whole thing.

## Day 3: Prati → Vizzavona

This day started off horribly in rain, hail, and strong gusts. I found out the hard way that my shoes were not as waterproof as they had claimed to be (sending hate to you, Salomon), but fortunately in the latest episode of good fortune the day cleared up quickly and I power walked to the next refuge, Campanelle. I do not know if it was closed during my visit there, but I did not see a single person there, and every door was shut. No tents were pitched either. I was mildly disappointed as I expected lunch there, but I only had to walk a short distance more to reach Gîte U Fugone, which served a delicious omelette.

The hike after was easy, mainly propelled by the excitement of returning to civilization that is Vizzavona and being able to stay in a hotel.

### Hotel Le Vizzavona Review

Unless you are a hardcore enthusiast, I strongly recommend you shell out €130 to stay here, Hotel Le Vizzavona, for the night. The rooms are clean, meals are great, and the whole place serves as a great midpoint reset for the whole hike. They also offer laundry machines to use and there is a grocery store nearby for basic supplies.

## Day 4: Vizzavona → Petra Piana

The start to this day was the latest out of all seven at a shocking 8 AM. The breakfast offerings at the hotel were simply too abundant, I had to eat like a beggar seeing food for the first time in a month. I did know beforehand that the day wouldn’t be too long, as I started to have an idea of how quickly I could go compared to the official guide’s time (I concluded in the worst case, I would need two thirds of the time listed on the guide).

This day marked the beginning of the northern section, which I knew was going to be technical and scrambly. I do not remember this day to be super difficult though. I knew the upcoming refuge, Petra Piana, was also listed as one of the most comfortable, so I had something to look forward to and I powered on. I had lunch at Refuge l’Onda, which served an entrecôte with fries, well worth the €28.

### Refuge Petra Piana Review

Petra Piana did not disappoint. There were plenty of showers that were comfortable and sinks to wash your clothes. Dinner service was okay—the food was average tasting but portions were sufficient. The tents were pitched pretty closely to one another but thankfully I still managed to get a good night’s sleep in. Breakfast was simple as usual, and I shelled out money for it as usual.

## Day 5: Petra Piana → Ciottulu

Out of the 4 days in the north, this day was the most comparable to one in the south: it was less technical and more heads-down walking. I logged more than 35 km this day in 9 hours 30 minutes. I had lunch at Refuge de Manganu and powered through the rest which was pretty much as I had expected: flat, easy walking.

### Refuge Ciottulu Review

Ciottulu probably ranks as last or second to last out of all refuges I stayed at. This is because the tent I stayed in probably wasn’t properly set up, and on a windy night, this wasn’t the most pleasant feeling. I had the sensation I was the only mass keeping the tent grounded. Dinner was standard pesto pasta that was pretty well made, and breakfast was simple.

## Day 6: Ciottulu → Carozzu

This day was without a doubt the hardest out of all 7. It was one of the two “triple” days where I did 3 étapes, one of them being the toughest, walking at the highest point of the entire GR20, the Pointe des Éboulis at 2,607 meters.

I had a second late breakfast at Refuge Tighiettu, where I was told (at 9 AM) that it’d be tough to reach Carozzu that day. I knew I had to rush and that there wouldn’t be much more time to rest left on that day.

As expected, the next part was extremely scrambly and required the use of hands constantly. What was hardest for me was descending. Climbing down on all fours was difficult, and so was going down on loose rock. Being an inexperienced hiker myself, I felt like I was endangering those below me as the rocks I set into motion could have tumbled and hit them. It was here where two Germans taught me to dig my heels into the ground and lean forward, which made my descent much faster. However, I still got quite a lot of bruises on my limbs on this day from slipping and sliding a few times.

I started the next stage at 14h30, expecting I wouldn’t make the standard dinner time of 18h30 but remaining hopeful. You encounter some funny conversations when you start a stage this late:

> “You’re starting this late? Did you bring a headlamp?”
>
> “T’as fait la grasse matinée?”
>
> “Pff it’ll take at least (big number) hours, be careful”

I would have been concerned about the last type of comment if I hadn’t had the first 5 days to properly gauge my level.

Still, the last stage was tough. The climb was technical and there were various technical parts nearing Carozzu. It was this day where I started having a serious outer shin pain which I suspect was peroneal tendinitis. It was especially bad when descending and leaping. I began to feel concerned but also relieved that only one day remained.

Somehow, I managed to reach Carozzu by 18h30, and what was even luckier for me was that they offered a 19h30 dinner service, meaning I had some time to shower.

### Refuge Carozzu Review

I really enjoyed my stay here. Not having a hot shower was an unforgivable downside, but the dinner was great and tents were well set up. They also sell hard-boiled eggs here, which was a first for me on this trip. Being the last night in a tent, all I wanted to do was vibe and enjoy the endless views of the mountains.

## Departure

Calenzana had much more to offer compared to Conca. After the hike, being thirsty, I headed immediately to a Tabac, which did not sell drinks (??), but pointed me to the [supermarket](https://maps.app.goo.gl/Qf3yBn5EZf6mPhUz7) on the other side of town, where I almost finished a liter of water on the spot. I found out it was also the location of pick-up for my luggage deposited in Conca. Seeing it again in a supermarket storage room somehow felt surreal and I felt reunited with my old self.

I found a [place](https://maps.app.goo.gl/117BgLA47KKaSmKa9) to get an ice cream and figure out how to get to Calvi, where I would be staying for the night. I found this service that had an application, but I wasn’t sure if it would work. I requested it regardless, and it came within 15 minutes. I feel like I got very lucky though, as they happened to have a taxi in the area.

I checked into my hotel in Calvi, still processing being back in civilization.

## Conclusion

This was by far the hardest physical activity I’ve ever done. I saw comments before starting that doing it in 7 days was harder than doing an ultramarathon, and I can see why despite never having done one—I had to manage a high level of exertion for almost half of a full day for a week straight, all the while making sure I rested well enough, had enough to eat, had enough water on me, etc. During the first few days there was no end in sight, but having the hotel to look forward to really helped.

I would do this trip again, but not in the near future. Some things I might do differently:

- I enjoyed doing it solo but would appreciate having a person to do it with.
- I might do it north → south to appreciate the hike from a different perspective.
- I might try to do it faster (6 days?), assuming I get more hiking/camping experience by then.
- I might go later in the season in September, as the August heat made the hike more difficult.
- Maybe add a few days to the end of the trip to do nothing and chill by the beach. Calvi and Porto-Vecchio were both great places to chill (just make sure you rent a car).

## Appendix

### Random Thoughts / Observations

- One of the hardest parts of this hike was adjusting to sleeping in tents, since I had no camping experience and value some level of creature comfort.
- That being said, this whole experience felt very “authentic”. I had to manually call taxis; there was no cell service for most of the hike; you could only pay in cash in most refuges; and most hikers were French rather than the nationalities that tend to overflow tourist spots. This was very unlike the Alta Via 1 which I did last year.
- This was as much of a mental challenge as a physical one. Unlike a marathon, the end does not come in hours, but days. Early struggles can really test your mental strength.
- I met people that stay wherever they end up at night, and I even encountered refuges (in late August) that did not verify my reservations. If done out of peak season, one could start the trip without booking tents in advance.
